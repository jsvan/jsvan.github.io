The Chemnitzer concertina is a German squeezebox with 52 buttons, and every one of them plays two different notes: one when you push the bellows in, another when you pull them out. C is here on the push, over there on the pull, and on the other hand an octave up somewhere else entirely. Players learn it from a fingering chart, a page of little circles with two notes drawn on a staff inside each one, and a caret over the push note. That is a lot of squinting before you get to play anything.

So I put the chart into the browser. Every button from Theodore Kloba's chart is entered by hand, with its push note and its pull note, laid out by row for each hand the way they sit on the instrument. Click a button and it plays its note. Flip the toggle and the whole board flips to the other bellows direction. Another toggle swaps the labels from button numbers to note names, so you can see the instrument the way it actually sounds.

The heart of it is the scale player. Pick one of the 12 keys, major or minor, push or pull, and it walks the scale up through both hands at once, the right hand two octaves above the left, lighting up each button as it goes. That is where the instrument shows its strangeness. In one bellows direction a note simply might not exist, so the scale stumbles and skips a step, and you see why players change direction mid-phrase. There is a slider to scrub back to the spot where you lost the pattern, and a chord button that lights up every root, third and fifth on the board.

It is plain HTML, CSS and Javascript, and the sound is a bare sine wave from the Web Audio API. It sounds nothing like a concertina. Real samples would help, but the point was never the sound, it was seeing where the notes live.
