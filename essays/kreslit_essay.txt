
Why I built this
Are the fantastic ideas locked away in essays more consumable if we change the form of their delivery? I wanted to see AI transform dense articles into an embodied flow of scenes where the ideas are spoken directly by their historical characters. For any topic of discussion, figures argue for themselves and against others. Maybe in the modern high-velocity, low-impact “doomscrolling” environment today, we can get better ideas into the populace. But in reality, I made it because I was too braindead after a day at work to focus on reading a long article. And I wanted to experiment with TTS/image/other AI generation companies.

Each article here is 95% AI-generated, allowing for creation of educational material at scale. The remaining 5% is hand-placing the sprites around the scene, which can be as quick as 10 minutes. The pipeline itself took months of hand-tuning (which is a figment of the 2025 LLM era); the per-article work is what's automated. I also compress the articles. I get scripts down to 50-60% of the original article’s total word length while keeping the same core arguments.

An example article I think that turned out well can be found at https://kreslit.com/?article=reflections-on-the-question-of-war-guilt  or https://kreslit.com/?article=the-idea-of-education-and-its-relevance-today .
The writing pipeline
In making this I struggled with AI to produce a decent output. There is a fundamental tradeoff between truthful allegiance to the text, explaining the ideas fully, not going overboard with synthesized slop drama (which ties into conciseness) and style. The most effective way of dealing with this is to break generation into many atomic stages. One stage handles argument extraction, another collects them into scenes, a third handles casting, a fourth script writing, etc. In the cases where I needed creative ideas, allowing some stages to be creative also allows them to come up with bloated or bad ideas. It is better to let them overproduce, and pipe the material into a second editing/trimming stage, than to tweak the original generation to create perfect output. 

On the other hand, model choice is hugely impactful. What a 3-stage sub-pipeline would struggle to adequately generate, a larger model could one-shot. Lots of trial and error goes into finding the optimal models for each task. Each company's models behave differently. OpenAI is a cleaner creative writer and produces less 'slop' than Anthropic's. However it is much more likely to hallucinate arguments/characters/ideas that don't belong in the final version. Ultimately I have Anthropic handling the majority of the pipeline, and OpenAI producing the final script after all the outline is in place. In this, OpenAI is much more concise than Anthropic, but therefore is a bit dryer. Anthropic goes above and beyond to add drama and stakes into the characters. Currently I use OpenAI. All in all, it's best to use another company's model as critic and editor, as models tend to love their own writing styles blindly.

Repeated arguments over every scene was a problem. Every scene would reiterate the same ideas, sometimes fully copying sentences but not always. This bloated the script and made it very boring and tedious to work through. I tried editing passes to cut down reiterated material, but the false negatives and positives were worse than not running that stage. I tried ideas like vector similarity of sentences and removing duplicates, but there was no reasonable linear separator in similarity space. I tried edit-distance between sentences, but there were too many sentences that subtly conveyed the same point without repeating language. Why was this happening? I told the LLM to fully introduce every argument, but while each argument was independent, many would stem from one cause. Naturally this cause would be repeated in every scene. Additionally one argument would have multiple side effects and conclusions, causing that argument to be repeated as well. What caused problems at the later stages stemmed from earlier stage output -- often too verbose, too much information, too much verbosity. Limiting what each stage sees is more effective than just a well crafted prompt. 

The most serious help in this would be having somebody with a background in writing who could diagnose the problems in a deep sense, finding systematic issues with the LLM’s interpretation, and shortening the experimentation development cycle.  
Casting and Scenes
I want to point out the balance it takes to get scenes that are not too thin (a couple characters, maybe one line each) but also not too dense (seven characters reciting essays). When should scenes split? When are scenes redundant? And boy, how much the LLMs want to assign the author/narrator as the central speaker with 60-70% of all the lines. One long monologue! Beyond this, casting was non-trivial. Who should be characters? When should fictional characters be created? Sometimes too many fictional characters pollute the story and real characters, who are directly referenced, never appear. When real characters did appear, and this was more a problem with OpenAI, they would often steal quotes from other characters or criticize their own belief system they were meant to staunchly defend. 

Also, somewhat humorously, when I encouraged a tight cast of characters to not have a number of one offs (which would be very expensive for generation and also didn't feel as cohesive), the LLMs got around this by making the characters the same in name only. For example, a "Marxian Schoolteacher" would replace Karl Marx, and be a man in one scene, a woman in another, German in one and Chinese in another, etc. Whatever combination the ideas needed to make the argument. In each scene they would reintroduce themselves with a different backstory. 
Images
In image generation, I use Replicate. In some basic tests it handled the LLM-generated image prompts better for my project. Getting a consistent art style is somewhat challenging, but with reference images, it works very well. Occasionally though the reference images would have too much impact, leading to every produced image mirroring the reference. For example, one batch of characters used a generated female character image with a long hair braid. This caused half the other characters, including males, to have braids and the same face. Moving on, I decided that characters switching through static poses are more fun than static sprites. 

Getting rid of the backgrounds of all the sprites was a bit of a struggle. I started with an algorithmic background remover that finds the most common pixel color (usually green), and greedily removes contiguous pixels within a small range from seed points around the image. For object sprites this works well enough, however for character sprites this often ended up leaving pockets of green. So for characters I transitioned to Replicate's API service for removing backgrounds. Great results, however occasionally the insides of characters are made transparent as well. Fixable with a sprite regeneration, but that’s more human work.
Voice
Voice generation with TTS was a huge pain. Few services allow an LLM-generated description to produce a matching voice. ElevenLabs allows this (at scale) at higher price tiers, and has great-sounding outputs. Their price was way too high so I searched further. Fish Audio is what I used for a while, but I was algorithmically scraping through libraries of user-generated voices, most of which were inappropriate as they were of anime or video game characters. I finally landed upon Hume, which handles everything perfectly for my use case, but they have since deprecated their TTS services and are pivoting their business. 

One overarching problem is speech speed. Models produce speech that's a bit too slow, but not uniformly so. Some lines or voices will be faster than others, and slow speech can really make it boring. I tried algorithmically speeding up speech by looking at the ratio of line length and the number of words in that line. However the TTS models occasionally add extra words to their line, or concatenate two lines together, which leads to unfairly off balanced ratios and accelerates the speech too much. Voice and image generation also had trouble with censorship: people and politicians, or for example Nazi scenery, would be blocked and I'd have to find more generic replacements.

Interaction
The experience that’s the most fun is clicking around. Clicking on people to see them make a pose and talk, exploring between scenes... but how else could I use this interaction? For a while I made a library of atmospheric sounds (computers typing, tanks shooting, birds squawking, glass breaking) and added easter egg click zones around each scene to reward random clicking. This was high-labor, low-impact, so I removed it. I added a boss fight questionnaire to the end to give some kind of reward for listening to all the characters. 
(Mini) Games
I added LLM-generated mini games. I tell the AI to imagine what kind of minigames could help educate the material and I hand it a list of game templates. The templates are previous LLM generated minigames that are saved for future ones to base theirs on. Templates work out very well. While I haven’t spent the time necessary, I find that I can iterate once on a template to fix bugs and aesthetics, and those changes carry forward to all future uses of that template. 

I also thought it would be awesome to turn this game 3D and explore it like in an MMORPG. Users could walk through galleries of articles and interact with other users. I explored some image-to-mesh models such as SceneGen, which I spun up on Hugging Face, but it was a hassle. I think in another couple of months this will be trivial. Ultimately I decided the 3D lost some impact of the game and began to get too confusing. The phrase "keep it simple, stupid" ultimately killed it. 
If I pick this back up
I would rework the mini games; they have aesthetic errors and deeper bugs. I would also like to try out Google's music generation model to generate background music per article or by scene. My test library of 5 audio choices is getting pretty annoying. Also the script still doesn’t quite work. Do characters need to introduce themselves? Do they need backstory or synthesized examples to add weight? What makes it magic? The problem is how to do this when needed and not go over the top, a line LLMs still cannot hold tastefully.
Looking back
Overall this was a fun project, but wrestling with LLMs to produce non-obnoxious output took me months. I went through about 6+ iterations of different generations of model testing, which became super tedious, and I'm glad to be putting this project aside. Every model release would undermine the pipeline I had put so many hours into. At this point, it might be worthwhile to throw it all away and start from scratch and see what the latest models can accomplish on their own.
