Do newspapers in different cultures agree on the moral weight of concrete things, like a dog or a kitchen, and only disagree on abstract ideas, like privilege or merit? That was my guess. I think newspapers are a fair proxy for what their readers think, since they have to sell to them.

I trained a separate word-embedding model for each of 36 English-language outlets in 8 countries (the US, the UK, India, Pakistan, Nigeria, the Gulf, South Africa and Israel), and built moral axes in each one from the Moral Foundations Dictionary. Every outlet got bootstrap replicates and split-half controls, so I could tell real disagreement from noise. That came to 206 models.

After training, I have yet to do the analysis of the results to really answer my question. So far, my analysis is vague and tangential to my source question. Preliminary vibe coded analysis shows the hypothesis came out reversed. Concrete nouns diverge slightly more than abstract ones, not less. They pick up local moral context from what happens to them in each country's news: flood, farm, cop, ballot. The abstract words are mostly too generic to carry any moral color at all. Outlets also agree more within a country than across countries, and you can see the country blocks in the heatmap.
