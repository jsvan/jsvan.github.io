This project supported a research paper in computational geometry under Prof. David Mount of UMD to find an SVM algorithm to work in Hilbert's metric space: https://scholar.google.com/citations?view_op=view_citation&hl=en&user=ytb8SDMAAAAJ&sortby=pubdate&citation_for_view=ytb8SDMAAAAJ:roLk4NBRz8UC

Philbert is a Python library for computing and plotting objects in the Hilbert metric of a convex polygon, built during the exploratory phase of the paper (WADS 2025, with Aditya Acharya, Auguste Gezalyan, David Mount and Sunil Arya). 

Let Ω be a bounded convex domain in the plane. For distinct points p, q in Ω, the line through them meets the boundary at A (beyond p) and B (beyond q), and the Hilbert distance is half the log of the cross-ratio of these four collinear points:

d(p, q) = ½ ln( |qA| |pB| / |pA| |qB| )

It is the symmetrization of the asymmetric Funk distance. A few properties follow directly from the definition:
- Straight segments are geodesics, though in a polygon not always the only ones.
- The boundary is at infinite distance; along a chord, distance grows logarithmically toward either end.
- Cross-ratios are projectively invariant, so the metric is invariant under projective maps of Ω.
- When Ω is an ellipse this is the Klein model of the hyperbolic plane. When Ω is a triangle it is isometric to a normed plane with a hexagonal unit ball.

When Ω is a polygon with m sides, a metric ball is a convex polygon with at most 2m vertices, and every vertex lies on a spoke: a line through the center and a vertex of Ω.

The question in the paper is the linear SVM in this geometry. Given two separable point sets in Ω, find the line that maximizes the minimum Hilbert distance from the points to it. At an optimum, d + 1 point–spoke pairs (three in the plane) have their contact points on the separator, and the problem is LP-type. The planar Hilbert case runs in O(n log² m), and the Funk case in O(n log m).


Each visualization answers a question that is trivial in the Euclidean plane and is not here.
- **What does a ball look like?** Euclidean: a circle centered at p, the same everywhere. Hilbert: a polygon with up to 2m vertices on the spokes through p. It is not centered at p, it is large near the middle of Ω and flattened against the boundary, and its shape follows Ω: hexagons in a triangle, near-ellipses in a near-disk.
- **How does distance grow along a line?** Euclidean: linearly. Hilbert: it diverges logarithmically at both ends of the chord, and the rate depends on where on the chord you are.
- **Where is the tangent from a point to a ball?** Euclidean: a closed form in the radius and the distance to the center. Hilbert: a binary search over the vertices of the ball.
- **What is the nearest point on a line?** Euclidean: the foot of the perpendicular. Hilbert: there is no perpendicular. The nearest point is where ℓ crosses one of the m spokes through p, so the distance is a minimum over m candidates.
- **What does the margin around a line look like?** Euclidean: a strip between two lines parallel to ℓ. Hilbert: a region bounded by two polygonal chains whose vertices lie on spokes joining vertices of Ω on opposite sides of ℓ. The chains are not parallel to ℓ, and the vertex of Ω that governs each part of the boundary changes as ℓ moves or r grows.
- **Which lines are tangent to two equal balls?** Euclidean: the outer tangents are parallel to the line of centers at every radius, and the cross-tangents rotate smoothly about the midpoint. Hilbert: the cross-tangents swing and change which ball vertices they touch as r grows, and not monotonically.
- **Which line is equidistant from three points?** Euclidean: a closed form; with p and q on one side and the third point on the other, it is the line parallel to pq halfway to the third point. Hilbert: the line must be tangent to three balls of the same radius, and finding the radius at which such a common tangent exists is the core search of the algorithm.
- **What is the set of lines at distance r from a point?** Euclidean: the tangent lines of a circle, which the polar dual maps to a conic. Hilbert: the tangent lines of a polygon, which dualize to a polygonal curve, with two branches when the ball does not contain the origin.
What I visualized
- **The metric itself:** the cross-ratio construction, and Hilbert distance plotted against Euclidean offset along a chord, which shows the blow-up at the boundary.
- **Balls about points:** growing balls with their spokes, and equal-radius balls tiled across a triangle, a heptagon and a 48-gon. The last is close to the Klein model, and its balls are close to ellipses.
- **Primitives:** the tangent from an exterior point to a ball by binary search over its vertices, and the nearest point on a line. That nearest point is always where ℓ crosses a line through the query point and a vertex of Ω, which reduces a continuous minimization to m candidates.
- **Balls about lines:** the spoke construction, and animations of the margin region as ℓ slides, rotates and as r grows. Boundary points are colored by the vertex of Ω that determines their nearest point on ℓ, which makes the arcs and the moments when they change visible.
- **Common tangents vs. radius:** two equal balls with their four common tangents as r grows. The outer tangents barely move; the cross-tangents swing and change contact vertices.
- **Three support points:** points sampled at distance exactly 1 from a line, their unit balls touching it, and the line equidistant from three points.
What did not work
- **Constant tangent combinatorics.** Perhaps the contact structure of the tangents is constant in r. The radius animations showed otherwise, which is why the paper's planar algorithm searches over r, with the cross-tangent parameterized by the radius.
- **Binary search over spokes.** Searching over spokes instead of vertices fails for lack of monotonicity along the spokes.
- **Polar duality.** The motivation was precision: searching over the position of a line in the primal depends on numerical precision, while in the dual the space of candidate lines can be carved into regions where it is known in advance which spokes determine each point-to-line distance, with new points added by a sweep. Under the map (a, b) ↔ ax + by = 1, a line tangent to a ball becomes a point on the ball's dual curve. A line tangent to two balls becomes an intersection of two dual curves, and the max-margin separator is the common point of three dual curves at r equal to the margin. The animations show this cleanly, in the primal and dual side by side. As an algorithm it was unwieldy: balls that do not contain the origin dualize to two-branched, hyperbola-like curves that had to be assembled sector by sector. The published algorithm works in the primal.
