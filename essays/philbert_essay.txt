This project supported a research paper in computational geometry under Prof. David Mount of UMD to find an SVM algorithm to work in Hilbert's metric space: https://scholar.google.com/citations?view_op=view_citation&hl=en&user=ytb8SDMAAAAJ&sortby=pubdate&citation_for_view=ytb8SDMAAAAJ:roLk4NBRz8UC

Why I built this
Most geometry quietly assumes distance is measured with a ruler. The Hilbert metric throws that out. Pick any convex shape, and measure distance inside it by how your points sit relative to its walls. The walls end up infinitely far away, straight lines are still shortest paths, and circles turn into polygons that are fat in the middle and squashed flat near the edges. If the shape is a disk you get the Klein model of hyperbolic geometry, so this is a generalization of something people already find beautiful, with the corners left in.

The question we were chasing: if your data lives inside one of these shapes, and you measure with the Hilbert metric, how do you find the best line separating two classes of points? That's a support vector machine, the textbook maximum-margin classifier, dropped into a strange geometry. It became the WADS 2025 paper Support Vector Machines in the Hilbert Geometry, with Aditya Acharya, Auguste Gezalyan, Dave Mount and Sunil Arya: https://drops.dagstuhl.de/storage/00lipics/lipics-vol349-wads2025/html/LIPIcs.WADS.2025.3/LIPIcs.WADS.2025.3.html

Philbert was my sandbox for the exploratory phase. Before you can prove anything about a geometry you need intuition for it, and drawing Hilbert balls by hand is a good way to be confidently wrong. I wanted a library that computes everything exactly and just shows me. Click the image above for the full walkthrough: seventeen pictures and videos, from the definition of distance up to the separator, each with an explanation of what you're looking at. The code is at https://github.com/jsvan/Philbert
Balls around points
The first thing I plotted was Hilbert balls, a lot of them. Every corner of a ball sits on a spoke, the line from the center through one of the shape's vertices, so a shape with m sides gives balls with up to 2m sides. Put a ball near a wall and it flattens out against it. Inside a triangle every ball is a hexagon. Turn the shape into a 48-gon and the balls go smooth and elliptical, which is the hyperbolic plane showing through.
Balls around lines
For an SVM the shape that matters isn't the ball around a point. It's the margin: everything within distance r of the separating line. In Euclidean space that's a strip with parallel sides. Here it bends toward the corners of the shape, stitched together out of spokes that cross the line. I made movies of this shape while sliding the line, spinning it, and growing the radius, coloring each boundary point by which vertex of the shape it "belongs" to. The boundary breaks into a few arcs, each owned by one vertex, and watching arcs appear, merge and change owner was the first time the combinatorics of the problem felt concrete.

A nice consequence: the closest point on a line is always where one of the spokes through your point crosses it. A continuous minimization becomes a check over a handful of candidates.
Tangents that won't sit still
My first algorithmic guess was to grow balls of equal radius around the support points and look at the lines tangent to them. I assumed the structure of those tangents, which vertices of which balls they touch, would stay fixed as the radius grows, so you could figure it out once and solve. My TODO file from the time has the answer in caps: NOT constant. The outer tangents barely move, but the cross-tangents, the ones that actually separate the classes, swing around and jump between vertices as the radius changes.

I also tried searching over spokes instead of vertices, binary searching for the right spoke. That died too. The docstring I left behind says it best: due to lack of monotonicity on the spokes, this method won't work. Binary search needs something that only goes one direction, and spokes don't give you that. Dave had the idea of a coordinated binary search over the vertices of the balls. My stub of it describes the plan optimistically and then raises an exception that says "I don't want to deal with this." The planar algorithm in the paper turned out to be a grown-up version of that idea: a coordinated binary search over the edges and vertices of the balls, with the cross-tangent parameterized by the radius. The tangents not sitting still is exactly why the radius has to be part of the search.
A detour through the dual
Polar duality swaps points and lines: the point (a, b) becomes the line ax + by = 1, and the other way around. That's tempting for an SVM, because "find the best line" becomes "find the best point". Each support point's ball turns into a curve, a line tangent to two balls becomes a place where two curves cross, and the maximum-margin separator is the single point where all three curves pass through each other. Watching three growing curves meet at one point is still the clearest picture I have of what the separator actually is.

As an algorithm it went nowhere. The dual curves are built sector by sector (the code calls them Sektoren) and every edge case comes back to bite you, especially balls that don't contain the origin, which dualize into two-branched, hyperbola-like curves. We dropped it, and the published algorithm works entirely in the primal.
What made it into the paper
In the plane the optimal separator is pinned by three support points, and the problem is LP-type, so the whole thing reduces to a linear number of small primitive checks. The planar Hilbert SVM runs in O(n log² m) time for n points in an m-sided shape, the related Funk version in O(n log m), and there are bounds for higher dimensions too. Getting there meant ruling out a lot of tempting wrong turns first, which is what the rest of Philbert is.
Building it
Almost every test in the repo is a picture rather than an assertion. Tests drew random configurations and plotted them, and when one looked broken I froze its points into a regression test with a name like test_broken_weird_a. Vertical lines caused divide-by-zero problems, which I handled by swapping x and y and pretending the line was horizontal. When I came back to turn all this into a showcase, I found that the hack had also been flipping the caller's points and never flipping them back. Geometry code is like that: it looks right in nine pictures out of ten.
Looking back
Visualizing is the fastest way I know to kill a bad idea. Most of what lives in Philbert is ideas that didn't work, the constant tangents, the spoke search, the dual, and the pictures are how I found out quickly instead of after a week of algebra. The ones that survived made it into the paper.

If I pick it back up, I'd make the plots interactive so you can drag the points around yourself, and I'd like to see the Funk distance and higher dimensions get the same treatment.
